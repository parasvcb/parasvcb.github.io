How molecular changes shape protein and cellular function

When my PhD mentor first asked if I wanted to study splicing and its relationship to protein diversity, I was immediately intrigued: how could changes in exon patterns, which I initially perceived largely as additions or deletions, lead to proteins with different functions? Insertions and deletions in protein sequences were already well studied, and many can occur without abolishing the homology used to infer structure and function. But during the course of time, we encountered a more basic challenge: what exon patterns were actually present? This required distinguishing sequencing and annotation artefacts from plausible patterns. Since we aimed to understand routes to protein diversification, we built a de novo gene-wide representation system, using exons as anchors. We then expanded this across genomes to capture greater diversity and found patterns that differed across species and in their relationship with evolutionary constraints.

That question gradually became broader during my PhD: how does a change, small or large, in DNA, RNA, or protein become a difference in what the resulting protein or cell does. Such a change may be a genetic variant, the use of a different splice site, an amino-acid substitution, or a chemical modification of a protein. For example, phosphorylation of a single residue, Ser46, can shift p53 toward an apoptotic response after DNA damage (PMID: 17349958).

I have approached this problem by studying individual parts in enough detail to understand why a change has an effect and how that effect fits into the larger system. Most of my work is computational, in close interaction with experimental groups. In my work, experiments have established whether a change affects structure or function; computation has helped me examine molecular details, motions, and interactions that may explain why.

Proteins: when a local change affects the whole

I first approached this question through protein sequence, structure, and function.

In Mondal et al., we studied Y321, a residue involved in the structural changes required for Vibrio cholerae cytolysin to assemble and form a membrane pore. Experiments showed that changing Y321 impaired oligomerization and blocked subsequent pore formation (PMID: 33089544). I used MD and network analysis to examine how perturbing this site affected other dynamically connected parts of the protein. This was not trivial to infer and became my first experience of tracing a local residue change to a broader structural effect. During this work, we also highlighted other regions likely to influence the structural organization required for multimeric assembly. Later work from collaborators examined different residues, particularly Y194 in the cradle loop, a region highlighted in our analysis, and showed that interactions there help release and position the pre-stem for membrane insertion (PMID: 41202807), providing independent support for the utility of community-network analysis in identifying functionally relevant regions.

In a related problem, but within a very different system, I studied the molecular basis of strength in spider-silk protein. We changed the amino-acid composition of model β-sheet nanocrystals and pulled them using steered MD to understand fracture mechanics (PMID: 34151552). The changed amino acids were introduced with the aim of modulating the existing tensile strength. Our analysis showed that apparently stronger side-chain interactions did not necessarily increase tensile strength. Models with stronger side-chain interactions could still perform poorly if the backbone hydrogen bonds did not work cooperatively. In simpler terms, the smaller amino acids present in these regions appear to preserve a backbone geometry that allows hydrogen bonds to work cooperatively under load. Maintaining this geometry may therefore be more useful for future design.

At a larger scale, experiments from other groups later provided a distinct perspective on this problem. They showed that changes to recombinant spider-silk proteins could improve fibre strength, but the usefulness of a design also depended on properties such as protein production and the ability to form fibres (PMID: 36505976). This is why I am interested in detailing how one part of a protein gets influenced in presence of its other domains, disordered regions, and interaction partners.

RNA processing: how different protein forms can arise

I then approached protein diversity from the other direction and asked how the different protein isoforms of a gene arise in the first place.

A gene can produce several mRNAs through alternative transcription and alternative splicing. These transcripts can differ in the exons they contain, where an exon begins or ends, whether an exon or part of it is translated, and consequently in the protein sequence they encode. Yet transcript diversity is not equivalent to protein diversity. Many transcript-level changes may not produce stable or functionally distinct protein products, and detecting individual protein isoforms remains difficult (PMID: 39778413).

The first problem we encountered was how to represent different transcripts and their encoded protein sequences consistently. In the literature, transcript isoforms are often compared in pairs, which is useful for understanding how one transcript differs from another. But when many transcripts need to be compared to understand gene-wide patterns, repeated pairwise comparisons become difficult to interpret, as the same exon may occur in several transcripts, its splice boundaries may change, and the same genomic region may contribute differently to the coding sequence. A standard multiple sequence alignment (MSA) might appear to offer a simple solution, but repeated sequence, shifting exon boundaries, and different exon combinations make exon correspondence non-trivial. More importantly, sequence alignment alone does not retain the genomic identity of an exon or show how that exon contributes to the respective protein sequence. We also explored MSA with genomic-coordinate information, but this still did not provide the exon-centred representation we needed.

To make this more tractable, without losing this information, ENACT gives exonic loci and their variants a consistent description within the gene. We record where an exon occurs, whether its splice boundaries vary, whether it contributes coding sequence, and how these changes affect the resulting protein sequence (PMID: 40335152). We applied this representation to protein-coding genes from five species and developed ENACTdb so that these relationships could be examined systematically (PMID: 39569321). Event-based approaches such as AStalavista had already provided rigorous ways to identify complex alternative-splicing events through transcript comparisons (Sammeth et al., 2008; PMID: 18688268). ENACT addresses a different gap by centralizing the exon locus itself, so that its splice-site variants and protein-coding consequences can be tracked across all transcripts of a gene. To our knowledge, this is the first framework to make the polymorphic nature of exonic loci explicitly trackable in a gene-wide, protein-linked representation.

For me, the value of this representation goes beyond exon annotation. Once the same exon and its variants can be followed consistently, it becomes easier to ask which parts of a gene remain constrained through evolution, where a gene repeatedly generates diversity, and which changes are specific to tissues or conditions. Most importantly for my future work, it provides a defined way to ask which RNA-level differences actually appear as different protein products.

I see two routes to extend this exon-level representation.

a) Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics: Recent studies show that splicing patterns can refine cell identities and reveal transitional states that are not readily recovered from gene-expression profiles alone, while long-read single-cell data show strong cell-type- and developmental-stage-specific isoform usage (Xiang et al., 2024; PMID: 38461306; Joglekar et al., 2024; PMID: 38594596). This creates an opportunity to test whether exon and splice-junction patterns captured through an ENACT-like representation can add information to the characterization of cell state and state transitions.

b) RNA and protein coorespondence: ENACT's present strength is in describing transcript diversity and its coding consequences. I next want to use this representation to ask which transcript changes are retained at the protein level and which remain primarily RNA-level events. This also meets a broader problem in omics: RNA and protein measurements do not provide identical descriptions of a cell. Some disagreement is technical, but some is biological: RNA can be translated at different rates, proteins can have different lifetimes, and one gene can produce protein forms with different structures or functions. Connecting these measurements would let me ask not simply how much RNA or protein is present, but which biological differences are preserved, altered, or lost between the two.

Disease variation and cell state

In my current postdoctoral work, I am applying this way of thinking to disease-associated variation and changes in cell state.

a) population aware variant interpretation. I am examining reported germline BRCA variation across Indian, Chinese, and predominantly European-ancestry datasets. For variants with limited evidence, I evaluate agreement among computational pathogenicity predictors as a prioritization signal. Preliminary experimental observations are now helping us test whether agreement among predictors can improve prioritization of variants for experimental assessment.

Parallelly to this, i am also understanding their molecular consequence using data driven approaches

b) DNA damage repair and protein state. I am now bringing my earlier structural work into DNA-damage repair. Genetic variants or regulatory changes can alter the abundance, structure, interactions, or assembly of repair proteins. I am particularly interested in whether disease-associated changes shift their conformational distributions, change their preference for interaction partners, and thereby influence repair-pathway choice or the cellular response to damage. Structural and single-molecule studies already show that protein and DNA conformations can influence successive steps of repair and contribute to pathway choice (PMID: 39260099; 39724838).

I am developing these questions in cancer and immune dysregulation as of now, where altered regulatory, repair, and protein states can directly affect cellular behaviour.

Additional collaborative genomics

Alongside this main trajectory, I have also worked in collaborative genomics. In Nasonia oneida, I contributed to the annotation of its first reference genome and its comparative analysis with other hymenopteran species (PMID: 40593889).

Where this leads

My strength is in gaining contextual depth within a system, whether physiologic or disease process, or one of its specific modules. This curiosity drives me to adapt and develop methods at the appropriate resolution. I now want to use this to determine which molecular differences persist from RNA to protein, which alter protein interactions or conformational states, and when these differences become important enough to contribute to changes in cellular or physiologic state. This connects the molecular variation I can characterize well with the broader question of how cells and biological systems differ, respond, and change over time.
