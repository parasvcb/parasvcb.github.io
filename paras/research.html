My approach to science

Biological systems have evolved diverse functional outcomes through connected layers of regulation, yet these outcomes can depend on surprisingly local molecular changes. A single phosphorylation site, for example, can alter protein activity or partner recognition. During the DNA-damage response, DYRK2-mediated phosphorylation of p53 at Ser46 promotes p53AIP1 expression and apoptosis. (PMID 17349958)

This complexity has also shaped how I have approached biology. I have had the opportunity to study several interacting biological modules individually and in depth. I see cellular states as coordinated across layers of gene expression and regulation, but believe that studying individual modules at mechanistic depth remains necessary. My research so far has involved decomposing complex systems into simpler modules, understanding them well, and reconnecting them into larger systems.

I have therefore explored different aspects of biological systems through a computational-first approach in close collaboration with experimental groups. This has shaped the way I prefer to work. Experimental constraints help guide computational interpretation and establish its biological relevance.

Core

I believe our strongest work often comes from developing a defined core capability and extending it when another perspective is needed to understand a biological system more completely.

My core concerns proteins and the ways their function can be diversified through sequence, packing, interaction networks and structural organization, as well as RNA processing that changes the protein products a gene can produce. This core developed during my Ph.D. and continues to evolve as the RNA and protein aspects of my work converge.

I use extensions to describe the broader biological contexts in which I apply and develop these capabilities. These include genetic variation, DNA-damage repair, cell state and communication between cells.

Protein systems

The protein systems I have studied connect residue composition, packing and conformational organization with different aspects of function.

In Mondal et al., we studied the contribution of Y321 in the hinge region to the structural rearrangements required for oligomerization and pore formation. Mutation of this residue impaired oligomerization and blocked subsequent pore formation. (PMID 33089544) In my contribution, molecular dynamics and community network analysis helped examine the effects of a local perturbation on dynamically connected regions. What fascinated me was the position of a single residue within a larger structural network, with consequences extending through the assembly process.

Later structural and functional work from collaborators established that cradle-loop residues, particularly Y194, contribute to pre-stem membrane insertion and pore formation. (PMID 41202807) This supports the importance of interactions surrounding the pre-stem region. The later study examined different residues from our Y321A study.

In Verma et al., we studied the molecular constraints relevant to modifying the strength of spider silk. We compared model β-sheet nanocrystals with different amino-acid repeats and examined their response to mechanical pulling. (PMID 34151552) That work made me truly appreciate the resolution molecular dynamics can provide. The organization of hydrogen bonds during pulling was particularly important. Stronger side-chain interactions did not necessarily improve backbone hydrogen-bond cooperativity or mechanical strength.

Subsequent experimental work by other groups engineered the poly-alanine regions of recombinant spider-silk proteins and measured the mechanics of spun fibres. Some substitutions improved fibre strength, while other constructs had problems with solubility, spinning or extensibility. (PMID 36505976) These fibre-level experiments differ from our simulated nanocrystals, but the comparison reinforces an important lesson for me. Sequence design must account for several interacting constraints, including the organization of backbone hydrogen bonds under load.

I would like to use simulations to guide selective substitutions while monitoring backbone hydrogen bonding, followed by experimental assessment of spinnability and fibre mechanics.

This also made me increasingly interested in multidomain organization and intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). Domains are useful units of structure, evolution and function, but a multidomain protein’s behaviour can depend on the geometry and communication between those units. (PMID 15093836; 32066080) In silk, β-sheet nanocrystals are embedded in an amorphous matrix that contributes to extensibility. (PMID 36505976) These different forms of organization strengthened my interest in the influence of a structural unit’s surroundings on its function.

I find a combined computational and experimental approach particularly useful here. Experiments constrain the biologically relevant states, while computation can examine their organization and interactions. This combination is also important in studies of protein allostery. (PMID 40043838)

Transcript diversity

In parallel, I was developing an understanding of alternative splicing and the diversity of protein products from coding genes. Alternative splicing can change the regions included in a protein and affect its structure, interactions and function. The relationship between transcript and functional protein diversity is not one-to-one, and isoform-specific protein detection remains challenging. (PMID 39778413)

We tried to improve the representation of the problem itself. I developed a systematic framework for annotating exons in a gene-wide manner. (PMID 40335152) Pairwise transcript comparisons remain useful, but a gene-wide representation makes recurring variation and its coding consequences easier to examine. I am also interested in the relationship between evolutionary constraint and a gene’s capacity to diversify its products.

After studying transcript-diversification patterns across five species and refining the representation over several years, ENACT provided a consistent way to annotate exon attributes within genes and transcripts and track their relationship to protein-coding sequence. (PMID 40335152; 39569321) The annotation retains information about exonic position, splice-site variation and coding consequence. Transcript diversification involves alternative splicing and alternative transcription, while changes in coding boundaries can also alter the resulting protein sequence. ENACT preserves these distinctions in its representation.

So far, much of this work has focused on annotation models and the patterns that become visible once exon variation is tractable across genes and species. This provides a basis for examining changes in protein products through splicing within an organism and evolution across organisms. I want to combine this representation with experimental constraints to characterize protein-family diversity and tissue-specific processing patterns.

I am increasingly interested in discordance between RNA and protein measurements. These measurements provide related but non-redundant views of gene expression, and single-cell protein measurements have distinct opportunities and limitations. (PMID 32709985; 33653632) For me, the challenge is to distinguish technical disagreement from regulation involving translation, protein turnover, proteoform composition or molecular states that gene-level measurements cannot resolve.

These projects are different on the surface, and I remain grateful to the mentors, friends and collaborators through whom I learned each system. They have also exposed a recurring difficulty in biological representation. What we conveniently measure is not always the unit through which function is carried out. A residue acts in a network, a domain in a multidomain context, and a transcript does not automatically imply a functional protein. A gene can produce molecular products with different behaviour. Much of my future work concerns this mismatch. I want to improve the representation, identify relevant constraints and combine computation with experiments to understand the contribution of molecular variation to biological behaviour.

Extensions

These capabilities have developed further through collaborative work in genomics and through my postdoctoral work on human variation. DNA-damage repair and its cellular consequences are becoming a particular interest.

Alongside this, I also worked in the genomic space, which, in a simple sense, completed the genome, RNA and protein circle of my training. In a collaborative project on Nasonia oneida, I contributed to the annotation of its first reference genome and the resulting comparative-genomic resource. (PMID 40593889)

In my ongoing postdoctoral work, I have extended this toward population-aware human variant interpretation by comparing reported germline BRCA variation across Indian, Chinese and predominantly European-ancestry datasets (work communicated). For understudied variants, this also involved comparing computational pathogenicity predictions. I treat these predictions as prioritisation tools rather than established pathogenicity, and prioritised variants are being taken forward for experimental assessment with collaborators.

I am interested in the conformational states of DNA-damage-repair proteins and the effects of variants or regulatory changes on interaction surfaces, partner selection and assembly. I want to examine their influence on repair-pathway choice and cellular state, followed by their possible consequences for communication with immune cells and surrounding tissue. Therapeutic response is a longer-term motivation for this work.
