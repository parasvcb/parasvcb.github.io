How to make a reader care about a problem, follow an argument, and understand what a result changes.

A practical masterbook built around close readings of public writing from Peter Swain’s laboratory, followed by original lessons and worked examples from computational biology.

Prepared for Paras Verma · 19 September 2026
14 chapters + orientation · Exercises, model answers, and a four-week practice plan

00 · Orientation

Learn the decisions behind the prose

The attraction of clear scientific writing is not simply that the words are easy. It is that the reader can see why one sentence follows another. A problem becomes a question; the question makes an approach necessary; the evidence changes what can be said.

This book treats Peter Swain at the University of Edinburgh as the reference you intended. It examines laboratory webpages and selected coauthored papers. Those texts do not establish who drafted each sentence, and they do not constitute a published writing doctrine. “Observed move” means a feature of the cited text. “Practice principle” means my interpretation or teaching advice. The grant chapters are an adaptation, not an analysis of his private grant applications.

What to borrow

Borrow the habit of asking what the reader needs next. Develop your own expression, examples, and rhythm. There is no benefit in making your work sound as though someone else wrote it.

Three ways through the book

  • For your profile: read chapters 1–5 and 10; finish with a 100-word introduction.
  • For a manuscript: read chapters 3 and 6–8; build a claim-and-evidence outline before polishing sentences.
  • For a proposal: read chapters 3, 7, and 9; turn each uncertainty into a decision the project can resolve.

All ENACT, proteoform, population-variation, and ALS passages labelled “practice” are original teaching examples. Proposed experiments and numerical results are hypothetical. They are not findings from your research or claims made by Swain’s group.

01 · Close reading

Observe what the writer makes easy

A. An opening that gives the reader an object

“We study how cells make decisions.”

Short quotation from the Swain laboratory homepage.

Observed move. The opening names the actor and question before explaining the biochemical machinery. Subsequent sentences connect the question to interacting molecules and disease. The reader gets a destination before a technical inventory.

Practice principle. Your opening should let a reader form a question, not merely recognise a field. Try replacing “My research integrates…” with an actual thing you want to understand. This is an entry point: the precise definitions still need to follow.

B. A dilemma that creates forward movement

“Speed undermines accuracy”

Short quotation from the stress-response highlight on the laboratory research page.

Observed move. That highlight uses a familiar sensory experience to introduce competing demands, then asks why yeast have two sensing pathways. Another highlight moves from an everyday response to changing surroundings to intracellular motion. These are explanations built around a problem, rather than lists of techniques.

Practice principle. An analogy earns its place when it clarifies the relationship you need. State where the comparison stops. In your work, a gene is not literally a recipe and a cell does not literally deliberate; useful shorthand must eventually give way to measurable terms.

C. A paper that takes the obvious alternative seriously

Observed move. In the 2022 transporter paper, Montaño-Gutierrez, Correia, and Swain ask why multiple transporters are needed when a single effective one seems plausible. The introduction considers alternative explanations before developing a rate–affinity account. Modelling and yeast observations have distinct roles; consistency with a prediction is not presented as unrestricted proof.

Close reading of the abstract and introduction in the 2022 transporter study.

Practice principle. Give the strongest simple alternative enough space to become a real contender. Then show what evidence separates it from your explanation. A paragraph that treats alternatives fairly makes the proposed test more interesting and the conclusion more credible.

D. A methods paper that explains why a method is needed

Observed move. The 2016 Gaussian-process paper begins with the scientific quantity to be estimated. It explains limitations of existing approaches, introduces its method, and states an assumption about measurement noise. Different biological examples support its proposed breadth. The technique arrives as an answer to a need.

Close reading of the abstract and introduction in Swain and colleagues’ paper on estimating time derivatives.

Practice principle. Explain which inference becomes easier, more reliable, or newly possible. “We developed a pipeline” says what you made. The reader also needs to know which decision the pipeline improves and how you checked that improvement.

What a useful answer looks like

“Defines why the quantity matters”; “shows why the current estimate fails”; “states the alternative and its assumptions”; “describes the test of usefulness”. Labels such as “background” and “more background” tell you very little about the reader’s journey.

02 · General writing

Give the reader a question they can hold

Readers can tolerate unfamiliar terms when they know what those terms are helping to explain. Difficulty becomes frustrating when the reader has to learn the vocabulary and discover the purpose at the same time.

Before drafting, finish two sentences: “My reader probably already knows…” and “By the end, I want them to understand…”. The distance between those answers determines what you must explain. A specialist needs less introduction to alternative splicing than a cross-disciplinary panel, but both need to know which uncertainty matters here.

Practice · Inventory first

My research integrates transcriptomics, proteogenomics, structural bioinformatics, and population genomics to investigate disease-relevant molecular complexity.

Practice · Question first

When RNA processing changes, which differences reach the protein? I compare transcripts and map their differences onto protein sequences and features.

The revision selects one opening question. It does not have to contain your entire career. Add the other research strands after the reader understands the centre of gravity.

The same project, three entrances

Define the term at the moment it becomes useful

“Proteoforms” may be a useful specialist term, but define the subset you actually analyse. Transcript-based protein predictions do not necessarily represent every molecular form present in a cell. A readable sentence should preserve that boundary: “We compare protein sequences predicted from annotated transcripts.” Introduce the broader term only when its scope helps.

03 · Questions and uncertainty

Make the uncertainty do useful work

A dilemma is more specific than “the system is complex”. It tells the reader why a sensible expectation is difficult to satisfy, why two explanations remain possible, or why an available measurement cannot answer the question.

You do not need to manufacture a paradox for every project. Sometimes the contribution is a reliable measurement, a careful resource, or a well-designed negative test. State that problem directly. Drama should come from what is genuinely unresolved.

FormOriginal practice questionEvidence the question asks for
Ambiguous consequenceTwo transcripts differ. Does the difference change the predicted protein sequence?Explicit coding-sequence mapping, including ambiguous cases.
Competing explanationsDoes an apparent expression shift reflect regulation within cells or changing cell proportions?An analysis capable of separating those explanations.
Measurement limitationCan short reads distinguish the isoforms needed for this claim?Identifiability checks or an additional measurement.
Practical trade-offCan a broad annotation system remain easy to audit?Coverage and interpretability assessments, separately defined.

Write the alternative before the hypothesis

Practice: “A change in exon use may remove a coding segment, alter an untranslated region, or leave the encoded sequence unchanged. We therefore classify the coding consequence before interpreting function.” The alternatives make the next action necessary. They also prevent “transcript change” from quietly becoming “functional change”.

Uncertainty needs a location

Compare “These findings may potentially be relevant” with “The sequence comparison identifies a predicted domain loss; its effect on ligand binding remains untested.” The latter is more confident about the observation and more precise about the unknown. It tells the reader where the evidence ends.

Model answer · hypothetical study

Uncertainty: an exon appears less frequently in disease samples. Explanation A: exon use changes within a cell type. Explanation B: the relevant cell type is less abundant. Discriminating evidence: a supported change within a comparable, independently identified cell population. Still unresolved: whether the transcript difference produces a protein-level effect. The exact analysis depends on the available data and their limitations.

04 · Sentence craft

Let actions carry the explanation

Plain language is compatible with precise mechanisms. The problem is not the word “mechanism”; it is a sentence that gestures towards one without showing what interacts, changes, or follows. Prefer “the substitution changed the predicted contact pattern” to “the substitution modulated the mechanistic landscape”, if the former accurately describes the analysis.

Four editing moves

  1. Give the sentence an actor. “We compared the models” is usually easier to enter than “A comparison of the models was undertaken”. Passive voice remains useful when the procedure or material is the natural focus.
  2. Put the action in a verb. Change “performed an evaluation of” to “evaluated”. Spend the recovered words on the comparison or uncertainty.
  3. Keep one main relationship visible. If a sentence contains the question, five methods, three systems, and an impact claim, split it according to those jobs.
  4. End where you want attention. Use the final clause for the new distinction or implication, not an administrative aside.
Practice · Compressed nouns

Population-aware transcript-to-protein consequence interpretation facilitates disease-relevant proteoform prioritisation.

Practice · Visible steps

We first ask which transcript differences alter the predicted protein. We then examine those changes alongside population-specific variant evidence to select candidates for further study.

The revision describes a hypothetical workflow, not a verified account of your project. “Select candidates” is a narrower claim than demonstrating disease relevance. If the two analyses are independent, do not join them this way.

Use rhythm to organise attention

A longer sentence can establish conditions and distinguish alternatives. A short sentence can state the consequence. Vary length according to the work each sentence does. A succession of short sentences can become choppy; a succession of long ones can hide the hierarchy of ideas.

Original example · Rhythm

Several transcripts may differ in their untranslated regions while encoding the same amino-acid sequence. Others may remove a coding exon or change the reading frame. The distinction matters. Counting transcript differences alone does not tell us how many different protein sequences are predicted.

Build a vocabulary of exact verbs

“Measured”, “estimated”, “predicted”, “classified”, “compared”, and “tested” describe different operations. Choose the one your evidence permits. Do not cycle through them merely to avoid repetition. Repeating a precise term can be clearer than introducing a near-synonym with a different meaning.

05 · Navigation

Let each paragraph answer the previous one

A paragraph is a unit of reasoning. Its sentences should do more than share a topic. They should establish, qualify, support, or resolve a point. The first sentence often tells the reader what to attend to; the last explains what that point makes possible or necessary.

1 · OrientWhat are we looking at?
2 · ComplicateWhat cannot yet be inferred?
3 · RespondHow will we address that?
4 · AdvanceWhat question comes next?
Original paragraph · An annotation problem

Transcript annotations describe different exon combinations, but those combinations do not always imply different protein sequences. A change may affect a coding region, an untranslated region, or the position of a splice site. To distinguish these possibilities, we map each difference onto the annotated coding sequence. This gives us a basis for asking which changes alter particular protein features.

  • Sentence 1: names the inference that is unsafe.
  • Sentence 2: makes the ambiguity concrete.
  • Sentence 3: gives the response to that ambiguity.
  • Sentence 4: opens the next, narrower question.

Transitions should name the relationship

“Furthermore” adds; “however” qualifies; “therefore” infers. None can repair a missing logical connection. Replace “Furthermore, structural models were analysed” with a reason: “To ask whether the sequence difference affects a known binding region, we compared its position in the structural models.” If that was not the reason for the analysis, write the real one.

A useful test: the sentence after “therefore”

Cover everything after that word and predict what should come next. If the actual sentence surprises you because a premise is missing, supply the premise or change the transition. A surprising result is valuable; a surprising logical jump is usually not.

06 · Evidence and results

Explain what changed in your understanding

A results section is not a diary of software runs. Readers need the comparison, the observation, and the inference it supports. Chronology matters only when the sequence of events is itself part of the evidence.

Build a result around five pieces

  1. Question: what did this analysis test?
  2. Comparison: against what baseline, condition, or alternative?
  3. Observation: what changed, by how much, and with what uncertainty?
  4. Interpretation: what does this observation support?
  5. Boundary: what does it not establish?
Invented numbers · For writing practice only

To test whether exon classification distinguishes coding from non-coding differences, we manually reviewed 100 transcript pairs. The workflow assigned the same category as the reviewers for 92 pairs. The eight disagreements involved ambiguous coding annotations. This agreement supports the classification in the reviewed set, while identifying annotation ambiguity as a source of error. It does not establish accuracy across all genes or show that the predicted changes alter protein function.

A real report would also explain how pairs were selected, how categories and agreement were defined, whether reviewers worked independently, and how uncertainty was estimated. Replace these invented numbers with verified results; do not reuse them as project evidence.

Separate the levels of claim

LevelExample statementWhat it requires
ObservationThe transcript lacks the annotated exon.Support from the stated data and annotation.
Sequence inferenceThe predicted sequence lacks this segment.A defensible transcript-to-coding-sequence mapping.
Feature interpretationThe missing segment overlaps the annotated domain.A specified feature source and coordinate mapping.
Functional hypothesisThe difference may affect ligand binding.A rationale linking that feature to binding.
Functional resultBinding changed under the tested conditions.An appropriate measurement and comparison.

Report a result that did not support the hypothesis

Practice: “The estimated difference was small, but the interval was wide enough to include effects of practical interest. The experiment therefore did not distinguish a negligible effect from an imprecisely estimated one.” This tells the reader more than “no significant difference was observed”. Absence of a threshold crossing is not automatically evidence of equivalence.

Make the figure do an argumentative job

A useful figure title can state a bounded finding. A caption should identify the comparison, units, sample definition, uncertainty display, and any statistical procedure needed to interpret it. A panel label such as “PCA” names an operation; the text should explain what the displayed separation does and does not mean.

07 · Significance

Earn the wider scope one step at a time

A wider implication is persuasive when the reader can trace a path back to evidence. “This could transform precision medicine” supplies a destination without a route. Specify the nearer change first: a classification that can be audited, a measurement that can be compared, or a candidate that can be tested.

FindingWhat did you establish?
CapabilityWhat can someone now do?
UseWho would use that capability?
Longer-term valueWhat further evidence is needed?
Original example · Resource significance

The classification makes the predicted coding consequences of exon differences explicit. Researchers can use those distinctions to select transcript changes for protein-level follow-up. Whether that selection improves experimental efficiency should be evaluated in a separate benchmark. Applications to disease studies will also depend on the quality and relevance of the underlying data.

This paragraph can support a meaningful resource contribution without pretending that resource construction is the same as clinical validation. It also offers a next study with an assessable outcome.

Use a limitation to refine the conclusion

“The analysis is limited” is too general to help. Name the limit, its effect, and the remaining conclusion. Practice: “Because the analysis uses annotated transcripts, it may miss unannotated isoforms. It can therefore compare the represented transcript models, but cannot describe the complete proteoform repertoire of a sample.”

Distinguish generality from universality

Evidence across different cases supports a broader domain of use. It does not erase the assumptions shared by those cases. Specify the domain: “across the evaluated species”, “under these simulated conditions”, or “for transcripts with complete coding annotations”. Breadth becomes more useful when a future user knows whether their case belongs inside it.

08 · Manuscripts

Build the paper around what the evidence can carry

Start with a provisional one-sentence conclusion and an evidence map. The conclusion can change as you inspect the figures. If a claim has no supporting analysis, remove it, narrow it, or identify the work needed to test it. Do this before you spend hours making the introduction elegant.

1. Title: choose a claim or a clear task

A declarative title works when the central finding is firmly supported. A descriptive title is often better for a resource, protocol, or exploratory study. Specificity is useful in either form.

Practice · Vague title

Integrated insights into the landscape of transcriptomic complexity

Practice · Descriptive title

Classifying exon differences by their predicted coding consequences

2. Abstract: a compact, complete argument

Include enough context to make the question intelligible, the specific problem, the approach, the main result, and the bounded conclusion. Follow the journal’s required structure. If the abstract contains results, give the actual principal result rather than saying that results will be discussed.

Abstract scaffold · Replace every bracket with evidence

Differences among transcripts do not necessarily produce different protein sequences. This makes it difficult to [specific interpretation task]. We developed [method or resource] to distinguish [defined categories] using [inputs]. In [evaluation set], the method [main quantitative result, comparison, and uncertainty where appropriate]. [Second result] identified [important boundary or pattern]. These findings support [direct conclusion] and provide [specific use], while [remaining limitation].

3. Introduction: narrow the question without overselling the gap

  1. Establish the relevant problem. Start close enough to the study that the reader can see the connection.
  2. Explain what current work already enables. Give credit and distinguish the available approaches accurately.
  3. Identify the remaining uncertainty. Explain why it matters for interpretation or action.
  4. State this study’s response. Clarify the question, approach, and contribution at the appropriate level of detail.

“No comprehensive framework exists” is a large literature claim. Unless you can substantiate it, write the actual distinction: “Existing annotations describe X; here we evaluate whether adding Y improves Z.” Do not invent a field-wide failure to make your contribution sound necessary.

4. Results: order by dependence, not by the folder structure

A possible resource-paper sequence is: define the representation; verify that it encodes the intended distinctions; compare it against an appropriate baseline; demonstrate an interpretable use case; identify failure modes. Each section should make the next one possible. The exact order depends on the evidence.

5. Methods: provide an audit trail

Specify data selection, versions, inclusion rules, mappings, parameters, comparison procedures, and uncertainty analysis sufficiently for the work to be understood and reproduced. Explain consequential choices. A software name is not a method description, and a repository link is not a substitute for describing the analysis in the paper.

6. Discussion: answer, compare, qualify, extend

Open with the answer your study supports. Relate it to the relevant prior work, explain plausible interpretations, address alternatives and limitations, then describe a justified next step. Avoid repeating every result or turning the final paragraph into a promise of benefits that the study did not assess.

Original discussion opening · Hypothetical evaluation

The evaluation suggests that separating coding from non-coding transcript differences makes the annotation easier to interpret. The strongest evidence comes from the manually reviewed cases; the broader catalogue remains dependent on the source annotations. The next question is whether these distinctions help researchers select more informative protein-level experiments.

09 · Proposals

Turn uncertainty into a credible plan

A paper explains what evidence has changed. A grant explains why a question deserves investment and why the proposed work can answer it. The persuasive force comes from importance, a discriminating plan, and credible delivery—not from making the outcome sound guaranteed.

This chapter is original teaching guidance. For application structure, NIH’s section advice discusses Specific Aims and the Research Strategy. UKRI’s core questions provide a different assessment structure. The actual funding opportunity controls the required sections, criteria, and limits.

Begin with the reviewer’s decision

A reviewer needs to understand the question, why existing work does not settle it, what you will measure, how the possible outcomes will be interpreted, and why the team can deliver. Make those answers easy to locate. Distinguish a scientifically interesting uncertainty from a practical risk that could prevent any answer.

PaperGrant
What did we find?What uncertainty will we resolve?
What supports the conclusion?What evidence will distinguish the possibilities?
How does the result alter understanding?Why would either outcome be informative?
What limits interpretation?What might fail, and how will we still learn?

A worked opening

Practice · Promise without a test

We will deploy an innovative multi-omics platform to unravel proteoform complexity and discover transformative therapeutic opportunities in ALS.

Practice · A question with a route

Transcript changes in ALS datasets can suggest altered protein forms, but they do not establish which changes affect protein features. We propose to distinguish supported coding changes from annotation ambiguity and prioritise a defined set for follow-up. The project will test whether this approach produces a more reproducible candidate list than prioritisation based on transcript change alone.

This is a hypothetical proposal direction. It needs a verified literature rationale, identified datasets, an explicit baseline, feasibility evidence, and agreed validation resources before it could become an application.

Write aims as questions or outcomes, then specify activities

“Perform RNA-seq analysis” is an activity. “Determine which candidate coding changes are reproducible across independent datasets” is a question the activity might answer. Aims can be descriptive or resource-building when appropriate; they still need a defined product and a way to judge its value.

Hypothetical three-aim architecture

Aim 1 · Establish the reliability of the mapping

Compare transcript-to-protein assignments with an independently reviewed reference set. Define agreement, ambiguity, and coverage before evaluation. The output is an auditable mapping with explicitly unresolved cases.

Aim 2 · Test reproducibility of candidate selection

Apply the mapping to preselected compatible datasets and compare candidate stability with a stated baseline. Account for relevant technical and biological differences. The output is a reproducibility assessment, including conditions where the approach does not improve selection.

Aim 3 · Evaluate selected protein-level predictions

Use a feasible, agreed assay for a limited set of candidates. If discovery produces no suitable candidates, evaluate preselected benchmark cases to test the mapping’s predictive limits. Benchmark testing addresses method performance; it does not replace disease-specific validation.

Make milestones assessable

A useful milestone names the deliverable, evaluation rule, timing, and decision. Practice: “By [month], complete blinded review of [justified sample]; estimate agreement and uncertainty using [procedure]; proceed to candidate prioritisation if [prespecified criterion] is met, otherwise revise the mapping and report unresolved categories.” Fill these brackets with a defensible design, not an impressive-looking arbitrary threshold.

Write risks as branches in the argument

RiskDecision and responseRemaining value
Public datasets cannot support the needed isoform resolution.Evaluate resolution early; narrow to identifiable events or use a justified additional source.A documented boundary of what can be inferred.
Candidates do not replicate.Test prespecified technical and biological sources of disagreement.An assessment of portability; no claim of robust disease candidates.
Experimental validation is unavailable.Resolve access before submission or restrict the aims to work the team can deliver.A coherent computational study with appropriately limited conclusions.

Connect capability and resources to the work

Explain which previous contribution prepares you for each aim. Your experience developing an exon-classification framework can support a mapping task; it does not by itself establish access to every assay or expertise in every disease model. Name the complementary expertise, confirmed access, time, and resources the project needs. A budget is part of this delivery argument.

Write impact as a pathway with a user

Practice: “The project will release an auditable candidate-selection workflow and a report of its failure modes. We will evaluate its usefulness with [identified users] through [defined task]. Further work would be required to establish whether selected candidates have disease-relevant functional effects.” Concrete outputs and adoption tests are more assessable than a promise of transformation.

Reviewer test

Could a reviewer explain what you would learn if the preferred hypothesis is wrong? Could they identify what makes the work feasible? Does a fallback preserve the original question, or silently substitute a different one? Revise until those answers are explicit.

10 · Research identity

Make a person visible through the work

A profile can sound personal without becoming informal. First-person verbs establish what you do; a concrete contribution establishes credibility; a current question gives the reader a reason to continue. Avoid making every sentence an attempt to define your entire research programme.

Original sample · Based on your supplied profile

I am interested in how differences in DNA and RNA processing change proteins. During my Ph.D., I developed ENACT to describe exon differences and connect them to protein features. I now work on population-aware variant interpretation and cell-surface proteins, alongside analyses of public datasets in ALS. Across these projects, I want to make the step from a molecular difference to a testable question more explicit.

Check that the description of your role and the relationship between projects are accurate before using this wording.

Choose one opening, then allow the work to branch

The first paragraph can centre on RNA-to-protein interpretation. A later paragraph can explain how structural modelling and population variation connect to that interest. If the ALS work is a separate collaboration, describe it as such. A coherent profile does not require a single causal story joining all your projects.

Write fit as a specific connection

For a potential collaborator or position, identify the group’s actual question and the contribution you could make. Practice: “Your work on [verified question] raises a transcript-to-protein interpretation problem that connects with my experience in [specific contribution]. I would be interested in testing [bounded next question].” The brackets require research, not flattering generalities.

Let personal detail remain concrete

“Outside research, I enjoy strength training and running” is sufficient if that is what you mean. You do not have to turn every interest into evidence of discipline, resilience, or an overarching philosophy. A detail can simply make the profile more recognisably yours.

11 · Revision and review

Revise the argument before the adjectives

Editing is easier when each pass has one job. If you try to fix the scientific logic, paragraph order, punctuation, and title simultaneously, easy local changes can distract from a missing comparison or an unsupported conclusion.

  1. Evidence pass: mark every claim and locate its support. Separate results from interpretations and proposed work.
  2. Structure pass: write a one-line description of each paragraph’s job. Move or remove paragraphs that repeat a job without advancing it.
  3. Reader pass: find undefined terms, unstated comparisons, missing premises, and sudden changes of scale.
  4. Sentence pass: clarify actors and verbs, remove redundant wording, and read aloud for rhythm.
  5. Integrity pass: check numbers, citations, names, contribution statements, figure references, and the applicable submission requirements.

Ask for a reader report before asking for edits

Give a colleague the draft and ask: What do you think the question is? What is the main conclusion? Where did you have to reread? Which claim did you find least supported? Their answers show whether the writing produces the understanding you intended.

Respond to reviewers with a traceable action

Original response example · Hypothetical revision

Concern: the manuscript appears to infer function from transcript changes.

Response: We agree that the original wording did not distinguish predicted sequence consequences from demonstrated functional effects. We have revised the relevant Results and Discussion passages to make that distinction explicit. The revised text identifies the affected feature and states that its functional consequence remains untested. [Insert the exact revised text and locations.]

Use “we have revised” only after making the change. If you disagree, state the scientific reason and address the source of confusion. Politeness helps, but it cannot substitute for a specific answer.

12 · Responsible assistance

Use AI to expose decisions you must make

AI can help identify where a paragraph is difficult, offer alternative structures, or make an editing pass easier to inspect. It cannot take responsibility for whether a claim follows from the data. Use it in a way that keeps your evidence, judgement, and authorship visible.

Ask for criticism before replacement prose

Reusable prompt

Read this paragraph as a scientist outside my immediate field. Identify the main claim, the evidence supplied, any undefined terms, and any place where the conclusion exceeds the evidence. Do not add facts or citations. Suggest a revision only after listing those issues, and explain what each change does.

Reusable prompt · Preserve claim strength

Edit for clarity while preserving the distinction between measured, estimated, predicted, and hypothesised statements. Flag any sentence whose meaning you cannot safely preserve. Return the original and revised wording side by side. Do not make the work sound more certain, more novel, or more general.

A practical workflow

  1. Start from your evidence and a draft you understand.
  2. Use only material you are authorised to enter into the chosen tool.
  3. Inspect the proposed changes against the original argument and sources.
  4. Verify every number and citation; test code before relying on it.
  5. Record the assistance and follow the current requirements of the destination.

UKRI’s AI application and assessment policy, checked for this edition, expects transparency about generative AI used in developing an application. Requirements can change and differ across funders and journals; check the relevant current policy for each submission.

A statement you can adapt

Draft only · Use if accurate

I use AI tools to assist with language editing and selected coding tasks. I review the resulting text, verify scientific claims against their sources, and test code before relying on it. Interpretations and conclusions remain my responsibility. I protect confidential material and disclose relevant use according to the requirements of the research output.

For a specific manuscript, describe the actual tool and use where the journal requests it. A general website statement does not replace a submission-specific disclosure. Do not add practices to the statement simply because they sound responsible; adopt them and describe them truthfully.

13 · Deliberate practice

A four-week writing course

Use one real project throughout. Work for about 30–45 minutes on four days each week. The aim is to make the argument progressively easier to follow, rather than to produce a larger volume of prose.

WeekFour sessionsDeliverable
1 · Question and readerClose-read an introduction; identify the reader; write three openings; define the central uncertainty and alternatives.A 120-word project explanation and a paragraph map.
2 · Evidence and expressionMap claims to evidence; draft one result; revise sentences; write a bounded significance paragraph.A 400-word results-and-interpretation sequence.
3 · Paper architectureOutline results; draft the introduction; build the abstract; revise a discussion paragraph and figure caption.A paper outline, abstract, and one complete result.
4 · Proposal and identityConvert the unresolved question into an aim; add milestones and risks; write a profile; obtain a reader report and revise.A one-page proposal sketch and a 100-word profile.

Keep a small revision journal

After each session, record one change and why it helped. “Removed jargon” is less instructive than “Replaced the methods list with the question, so the reader knows what the methods are for.” Over time, the journal becomes a record of your own writing decisions.

A practice rubric, not a scientific score

Rate each dimension from 0 to 2: 0 missing or confusing; 1 present but incomplete; 2 clear and supported. Use the scores to locate revision work, not to claim objective writing quality.

Capstone check

Give both versions to a reader without saying which is newer. Ask them to state the question and conclusion for each. If the newer version produces a more accurate understanding, that is stronger feedback than “it sounds polished”.
