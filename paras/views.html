Cancer as a multiscale biological system

I find it useful to approach cancer through two interacting perspectives. One begins inside malignant cells, with genetic alteration, epigenetic change, altered repair, metabolism and cellular plasticity. The other begins with the tissue ecosystem, including stromal organization, immune surveillance and escape, and communication among malignant and non-malignant cells. These processes can reshape one another. (PMID 35022204; 26241053)

Why immune responses become ineffective, suppressive or tumour-supportive cannot be explained reliably by a single mechanism. Immune selection, metabolic conditions and the cellular composition of the tumour microenvironment can contribute in context-dependent ways. (PMID 26241053; 30873592)

One analogy I find useful is Dvorak’s description of tumours as “wounds that do not heal”. Tumours and repairing tissues share features of inflammation, proliferation, migration and tissue remodelling. (PMID 3537791; 29958632) I find the analogy useful for considering why programmes that normally resolve after tissue repair can persist in a tumour. It is not a complete theory of cancer.

In heterogeneous tumours, clonal states and interactions with the microenvironment can influence progression and treatment resistance. Single-cell and spatial approaches can help investigate this heterogeneity and its spatial context. (PMID 39271263) I am interested in communication between tumours and surrounding tissue, including signals received and returned by apparently non-malignant cells, stromal populations and immune cells. I am currently reading single-cell and spatial studies and plan to begin analyses in November 2026.

I would also like to examine evolutionary constraints on the molecular features involved in this communication. Sequence conservation and protein structure can inform the interpretation of coding variation. (PMID 20399638) Their relevance to tissue communication will need to be evaluated in the specific biological context.

Agentic AI and biological representation

AI tools have already increased the pace of computational work for me. Agentic systems extend this through iterative feedback and tool use, including consultation of documentation that I previously read only briefly. In my experience, generating alternative explanations, recognizing an inadequate biological representation and choosing an uncertainty for experimental investigation still require scientific judgement.

In biology, particularly clinical biology, I think there is a limitation upstream of the AI itself. The most informative functional unit is not always clear for a given investigation. RNA, protein abundance, proteoforms and localization each capture part of a cell’s state. In my view, the choice of representation deserves as much attention as the agent’s ability to reason over it.

I see an opportunity for agentic computational biology in representing functional units more accurately across omics layers and modalities. I expect this could improve hypothesis prioritisation, expose contradictions earlier and make exploratory patterns easier to develop into experimental tests. These are benefits I would like to evaluate in my own work.

AI use and responsibility

I use AI tools to assist with writing, editing and computational work. They help me brainstorm around my ideas, improve expression and work more efficiently. I remain responsible for checking sources, reviewing analyses and code, and ensuring that the final work reflects my understanding. Where relevant, I disclose their use in individual research outputs.
