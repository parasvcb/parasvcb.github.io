Cancer as a multiscale biological system

I find it useful to look at cancer at two interacting scales. One begins inside malignant cells, including genetic changes, epigenetic changes, altered repair, metabolism and cellular plasticity. The other begins from the surrounding tissue, including stromal organization, immune surveillance and escape, and communication between malignant and non-malignant cells. These two scales are not independent. Changes inside malignant cells can influence surrounding cells, and the tissue environment can also select or change malignant-cell states. (PMID: 35022204; 26241053)

This interaction is also important for tumour immunity. Immune cells may remove some malignant cells while allowing or selecting others. Their activity can also change because of nutrient competition and metabolites, hypoxia, stromal cells and TME* composition. Which factor becomes more important can differ between tumours and also between regions of the same tumour. (PMID: 26241053; 30873592; 39798090)

One analogy I find useful is Dvorak’s description of tumours as “wounds that do not heal”. Tumours and repairing tissues share inflammation, proliferation, migration and tissue remodelling. (PMID: 3537791; 29958632) What interests me here is why processes that normally stop after tissue repair can continue in a tumour.

In heterogeneous tumours, different clonal states and their surrounding cell interaction can influence progression. Single-cell and spatial approaches now allow these states to be studied comprehensively with their tissue niche. (PMID: 39271263; 41331191) I am particularly interested in communication between malignant cells, stromal populations and immune cells, and whether apparently non-malignant cells retain information about their interaction with the tumour.

I would also like to bring my previous work on sequence variation and protein structure into this. Cell–cell communication is often inferred from expression of ligands and receptors, but expression alone is uninformative of which sequence variation, isoform differences or structural changes may alter the interaction. Sequence conservation and protein structure may therefore help prioritize which molecular changes are more likely to affect communication, although their importance will still need to be tested in the relevant biological context. (PMID: 20399638)

Agentic AI and biological representation

Agentic AI has made computational biology faster by connecting literature, code, databases and different analytical tools in one workflow. I see high value in this, but I think an important problem comes even before reasoning starts: how the biological question and its units are represented, which is yet pending even in non agentic workflows, where a gene-level RNA measurement dont inform isoform differences, and similar for protein abundance, proteoforms, and their influence in interactions or different conformations. For me, the choice of biological representation is as important as how capable the agent is at reasoning over it, because If an important distinction is not made in planning stage, better reasoning alone may not recover it.

With this, I see a useful role for agentic computational biology here. Different tools or agents could examine the same question at different levels(for example gene, transcript, protein, structure, cell or tissue). Instead of combining all information immediately, it may be useful to first identify where these representations agree and where they do not. I would like to test whether this can improve hypothesis prioritisation, identify contradictions earlier and help decide which uncertainty is most useful to examine experimentally.

AI use and responsibility

I use AI tools for writing, editing and computational work. They help me brainstorm around my ideas, improve expression and often (but not always) work efficiently. I remain responsible for checking sources, reviewing analyses and code. Where relevant, I disclose AI use in individual research outputs.
